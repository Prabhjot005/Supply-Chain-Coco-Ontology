Decision: How to split audit responsibility

REJECTED   Uniform streams + audit table for all 9 tables

Original plan: 9 streams + 9 tasks + 1 MASTER_DATA_AUDIT table = 19 extra objects.

Why rejected: Over-engineered. Master data (SUPPLIERS, PARTS, PLANTS, CUSTOMERS) rarely changes — dedicated CDC infrastructure is unnecessary overhead. Snowflake provides built-in alternatives that cover audit needs with zero objects to manage.

SELECTED   Split by data profile

Master tables (4): Access History — 365 days built-in, tracks who queried/modified, no objects to create. Sufficient for data that changes rarely.

Operational tables (5): Time Travel with DATA_RETENTION_TIME_IN_DAYS = 90. Shipments and orders complete within this window. Allows querying any historical state on demand.

Why selected: Right tool for each data profile. No custom infrastructure to maintain. Access History can't fail or go stale. Time Travel is always-on. Eliminates 19 objects (9 streams, 9 tasks, 1 audit table).

REJECTED   Time Travel for master tables too

Could set 90-day retention on SUPPLIERS, PARTS, PLANTS, CUSTOMERS as well.

Why rejected: Master data is active long-term — a supplier added 6 months ago is still relevant. Time Travel's 90-day max window doesn't cover the full lifecycle. Access History's 365-day retention is a better fit, and query-level tracking is sufficient for rare changes.

Decision: Refresh mode

REJECTED   FULL refresh (auto-selected by Snowflake)

Initial deployment: Snowflake auto-selected FULL for all 4 DTs due to CURRENT_DATE() usage and complex CTEs.

Why rejected: FULL refresh re-executes the entire query each cycle. It also prevents creating streams on DTs (streams require INCREMENTAL refresh for change tracking). Without streams, TASK_ORDER_RISK_ALERT had to use schedule-based polling instead of event-driven triggers.

SELECTED   INCREMENTAL refresh + view layer for time-dependent calculations

Removed CURRENT_DATE() from all DT queries. Moved time-dependent risk scoring to V_ORDER_FULFILLMENT view (computed at query time). Explicitly set REFRESH_MODE = INCREMENTAL on all 4 DTs.

Why selected: (1) INCREMENTAL processes only changed rows — faster at scale. (2) Enables streams on DTs — STREAM_ORDER_RISK now works on DT_ORDER_FULFILLMENT. (3) All 3 tasks are stream-triggered (event-driven, not polling). (4) Risk scores are always fresh because the view computes them with CURRENT_DATE() at query time.

REJECTED   Keep CURRENT_DATE in DTs and accept FULL refresh

Pre-compute risk_score, risk_level, days_remaining directly in DT_ORDER_FULFILLMENT.

Why rejected: Blocks INCREMENTAL refresh. Blocks streams on the DT. Forces schedule-based polling for order risk alerts. Risk scores would only be accurate at refresh time, not query time. The view layer solves all of these while keeping risk scores live.

V_ORDER_FULFILLMENT — view architecture

Decision: Audit for dynamic tables

REJECTED   Streams on dynamic tables for audit

Place a stream on each DT to track how aggregated metrics change over time.

Why rejected: Dynamic tables are derived data — auditing the source base tables is sufficient. Adding audit streams on DTs doubles the CDC infrastructure for no additional governance value. Exception: STREAM_ORDER_RISK on DT_ORDER_FULFILLMENT is kept, but for action triggers, not audit.

SELECTED   Time Travel (90 days) on dynamic tables

Allows querying past metric states: “What was the OTD% for Supplier X two weeks ago?”

Why selected: Zero infrastructure. Covers the operational lifecycle. Metrics older than 90 days can be captured in scheduled reports if needed.

Decisions: Which streams to keep

REJECTED   9 streams (one per base table)

Original plan: stream every table for comprehensive CDC.

Why rejected: Most base tables don't need real-time action triggers. Master data (SUPPLIERS, PARTS, PLANTS, CUSTOMERS) rarely changes. Detail tables (SHIPMENT_LINES, ORDER_LINES) are covered by their parent table streams. Audit is handled by Access History and Time Travel instead.

REJECTED   4 streams (SUPPLIERS, SHIPMENTS, INVENTORY, ORDER_RISK)

Intermediate proposal: keep STREAM_SUPPLIERS for procurement governance.

Why rejected: Supplier data changes are rare and don't require real-time action. Access History adequately tracks who modified supplier records. Removed to keep streams focused on high-frequency operational events.

SELECTED   3 streams (SHIPMENTS, INVENTORY, ORDER_RISK)

Only streams that drive operational actions: late shipment alerts, low stock alerts, and order risk escalation.

Why selected: Each stream is paired with a task that takes real action (Slack, Jira, expedite). No stream exists purely for logging. This is the minimum set needed for the event-driven pipeline.

Decisions: Task scope

REJECTED   9 audit tasks (one per stream)

Original plan: each task consumes its stream and writes to MASTER_DATA_AUDIT.

Why rejected: Audit responsibility moved to Time Travel + Access History. No custom audit table needed. Tasks should perform actions, not duplicate built-in Snowflake capabilities.

REJECTED   1 task (ORDER_RISK_ALERT only)

Intermediate proposal: only the risk alert task, since DT_ORDER_FULFILLMENT already aggregates signals.

Why rejected: Left STREAM_SHIPMENTS and STREAM_INVENTORY without corresponding actions. Those streams capture valuable operational events (late deliveries, stock drops) that should trigger immediate responses independent of the order risk pipeline.

SELECTED   3 action tasks (one per stream)

Each stream paired with a task that takes real action: alerts, Jira tickets, expedites, reorder triggers.

Why selected: Complete event-driven pipeline. Every stream has a consumer. Every task produces a visible business outcome. No orphaned streams or tasks doing redundant work.

Approaches evaluated

REJECTED   Custom audit table (MASTER_DATA_AUDIT)

Dedicated table logging every row-level change from all 9 base tables via streams + tasks.

Why rejected: Required 9 streams + 9 tasks + 1 table = 19 objects. Custom infrastructure that reimplements what Snowflake provides natively. Tasks can fail silently, creating audit gaps. Time Travel and Access History are always-on and maintained by Snowflake.

REJECTED   Time Travel for everything

Set 90-day retention on all tables uniformly.

Why rejected: Master data has a lifecycle longer than 90 days. A supplier added 6 months ago is still active. Access History's 365-day window and query-level tracking better suits rarely-changing, long-lived data.

REJECTED   Access History for everything

Rely solely on ACCOUNT_USAGE.ACCESS_HISTORY for all audit needs.

Why rejected: Access History tracks query-level access, not row-level changes. For high-churn operational tables (INVENTORY, SHIPMENTS), you need to see exactly which rows changed — Time Travel provides that capability. Access History alone can't answer “what was the stock level for Part #P100 at Plant Chicago two weeks ago?”

SELECTED   Hybrid: Access History (master) + Time Travel (operational)

Each data profile gets the audit method that matches its change frequency and lifecycle.

Why selected: Zero custom objects for audit. Right granularity for each table type. No infrastructure to maintain, monitor, or troubleshoot. Built-in features can't fail or go stale.

8. Skills

Prompt-based SKILL.md packages that guide the agent on how to analyze, interpret, and recommend. Skills don’t execute code directly — the agent uses Cortex Analyst and custom tools to carry out the skill’s logic. Published to SKILL_STAGE for reuse across agents.

Skills vs Custom Tools

Key distinction: Skills teach the agent how to think about a problem (flexible, analytical). Custom tools give the agent predefined actions to call (deterministic, safe). Analytical work → skill. Write actions → custom tool with guardrails.

Skill 1: supplier_scorecard

AttributeDetail
PurposeRank and evaluate suppliers by composite performance score. Flag underperformers. Recommend supplier for reorders.
Data sourcesDT_DELIVERY_PERF (OTD, lead time), DT_LANDED_COST (cost), SUPPLIERS (quality rating)

REJECTED   Separate skill + separate GENERATE_SCORECARD UDF (no connection)

Original plan had both operating independently — duplicating scoring logic in two places.

Why rejected: The scoring formula (OTD weight + quality weight + cost weight) is deterministic and belongs in a UDF. The interpretation (ranking, flagging, recommending) is analytical and belongs in a skill. Keeping them separate caused duplication.

SELECTED   Merged: skill calls the UDF, then interprets results

GENERATE_SCORECARD UDF computes the raw score. The supplier_scorecard skill calls it, then layers on analysis: ranking, comparison, flagging, and recommendations.

Why selected: Single source of truth for the formula (UDF). Rich analytical layer on top (skill). No duplication. The UDF is also reusable independently by other skills (e.g., reorder_advisor uses it to pick the best supplier).

Revised ETA calculation

Derived from historical SHIPMENTS data — no separate carrier table needed:

For each carrier + route combination: avg_delay = AVG(actual_delivery - expected_delivery) from historical shipments late_rate = COUNT(late) / COUNT(total) revised_eta = expected_delivery + avg_delay (if carrier tends to be late) Confidence level: HIGH: 50+ historical shipments for this carrier/route MEDIUM: 10-49 historical shipments LOW: fewer than 10 (disclose to user)

REJECTED   Separate carrier performance reference table

Create and maintain a dedicated table tracking carrier metrics.

Why rejected: Historical SHIPMENTS already contains carrier, expected_delivery, and actual_delivery. We can derive avg delay and late rate directly. Adding a reference table means another object to maintain and keep in sync.

SELECTED   Derive carrier performance from historical SHIPMENTS

Query SHIPMENTS WHERE actual_delivery IS NOT NULL, grouped by carrier, to compute avg delay and late rate per carrier/route.

Why selected: Zero additional objects. Always up-to-date (uses the same table being tracked). Confidence level disclosure when data is limited prevents overreliance on thin samples.

ML Forecast flow

REJECTED   Manual rolling averages and trend detection

Compute demand trends using simple 30-day rolling averages and percentage comparison of recent vs older periods.

Why rejected: Cannot detect seasonality patterns (weekly, monthly, quarterly). Provides only a point estimate with no confidence range. Custom statistical code to maintain. More prone to outlier distortion.

SELECTED   Snowflake ML FORECAST function (multi-series)

Uses built-in FORECAST with SERIES_COLNAME for multi-series support. One model trained once covers all part/plant combinations. Each query runs inference only — no retraining. Model is rebuilt on demand when staleness guardrail triggers. Falls back to rolling averages when insufficient training data (< 60 days).

Why selected: Single model object for all series. Train once, query many times. Automatic seasonality and confidence intervals. No per-query training cost.

REJECTED   Individual models per part/plant combination

Create a separate forecast model for each part_id + plant_id pair (e.g., demand_model_P100_CHICAGO).

Why rejected: Hundreds of models to manage and retrain. More granular control is not needed — Snowflake’s multi-series FORECAST handles per-series patterns within a single model.

Guardrails (demand_planner)

GuardrailBehavior
Minimum training dataML FORECAST needs at least 2 seasonal cycles. If < 60 days of history, fall back to 30-day rolling average and disclose: “Insufficient data for ML forecast — using rolling average instead.”
Confidence interval disclosureAlways show the range, not just the point estimate: “Projected demand: 1,140 units (range: 980 – 1,300).”
Outlier in training dataIf a single day’s demand > 5x the median, flag: “Training data includes a spike on Oct 1 (2,000 units vs median 200). This may inflate the forecast.” Let user decide whether to exclude.
Zero demand warningIf no orders in last 30 days, skip ML and say: “No demand recorded. Part may be discontinued or seasonal.”
Model stalenessIf model was built > 7 days ago and new orders have arrived, recommend rebuilding: “Forecast model is 10 days old — 45 new orders since last build.”
Forecast vs actual divergenceIf a previous forecast exists, compare against actuals: “Last forecast predicted 380 units — actual was 520 (37% higher). Demand may be accelerating.”

12. Semantic View (SUPPLY_CHAIN_ONTOLOGY)

Single governed semantic layer that encodes business meaning so Cortex Analyst returns consistent answers regardless of who asks or how they phrase the question.

Decision: Which tables to model

REJECTED   Semantic view on dynamic tables only

Model only the 4 DTs. Simpler — 4 tables instead of 13.

Why rejected: Cannot answer detail-level questions like “show me all shipments from Supplier X last month.” That data lives in base tables, not DTs. Limits the agent to aggregated answers only.

REJECTED   Semantic view on base tables only

Model the raw entities and let Cortex Analyst compute metrics at query time.

Why rejected: Complex metric calculations (like risk score with 4 factors) are hard to express as semantic view measures. Cortex Analyst has to reconstruct what the DTs already compute. Slower and error-prone.

Full ontology — base entities for detail queries, DTs for pre-computed metrics. Verified queries point to DTs for metrics (fast, consistent) and base tables for drill-down.

Why selected: Full coverage. “What is OTD for Supplier X?” hits DT_DELIVERY_PERF. “Show Supplier X’s last 10 shipments” hits SHIPMENTS. The semantic view routes to the right source. This delivers “any team asks cross-domain questions and gets one consistent answer.”

14. Role-Based Access Control (RBAC)

Column-level security using Snowflake masking policies. 3 domain roles restrict what each team sees. Primary agent has full read access to answer cross-domain questions. Sub-agents run under their domain role with masking enforced by Snowflake.

Decision: Primary agent access level

REJECTED   Restricted primary agent (SC_AGENT_ROLE with masking)

Primary agent sees only columns common to all roles. Cost, supplier ratings, customer details, safety stock, reorder points all masked.

Why rejected: Primary agent becomes near-useless — most of the 14 verified queries involve at least one masked column. Cross-metric queries fail silently. The agent spends most of its time routing instead of answering. The semantic view loses its value on the primary agent.

SELECTED   Full read access for primary agent, RBAC enforced on domain sub-agents

SC_AGENT_ROLE bypasses all masking policies (read-only, no DDL). The 3 domain roles (PROCUREMENT_ROLE, PLANNING_ROLE, LOGISTICS_ROLE) are restricted by masking policies. Sub-agents run under domain roles.

Why selected: Primary agent is infrastructure, not a user persona. It needs the full picture to answer cross-domain questions and route intelligently. The semantic view and all 14 verified queries work completely. RBAC is enforced where it matters — at the domain sub-agent level where skill-based analysis happens.

Decision: How to enforce column restrictions

REJECTED   Secure views per role (3 views per table)

Create role-specific views: SUPPLIERS_PROCUREMENT_V, SUPPLIERS_PLANNING_V, SUPPLIERS_LOGISTICS_V, etc.

Why rejected: Creates many extra objects (potentially 3 views per table = ~39 views). Semantic view would need to reference different views per role. Complex maintenance. Doesn’t scale.

SELECTED   Snowflake column masking policies

5 masking policies applied directly on base tables and DTs. No extra views. Semantic view references the same physical tables for all roles — Snowflake handles visibility at the engine level. Zero changes to semantic view YAML for RBAC.

Why selected: Native Snowflake feature. No extra objects. Works seamlessly with semantic view and agents. Security is a separate policy layer, not baked into the data model.

15. Streamlit App (Supply Chain Analyst)

One app, three persona views. Deployed to Streamlit in Snowflake (SiS). Persona auto-detected from Snowflake role with manual override for demos. NL query interface embedded on every page, backed by the Cortex Agent.

Decision: Deployment

REJECTED   Local Streamlit deployment

Run the app locally or on a separate server.

Why rejected: Needs separate authentication. RBAC must be managed in app code instead of leveraging Snowflake roles. Not shareable via Snowflake URL. Doesn’t demonstrate “working across surfaces.”

SELECTED   Streamlit in Snowflake (SiS)

Deployed to Snowflake. RBAC comes free — the app runs under the user’s Snowflake role, masking policies apply automatically. Shareable URL across the org.

Why selected: No separate auth. Masking policies enforce column visibility without app-level code. Matches the “working across surfaces” evaluation criterion (Snowsight Cloud). One-click access for any Snowflake user.

Decision: NL query backend

REJECTED   Cortex Analyst only (no agent)

NL interface calls Cortex Analyst directly for fast data queries.

Why rejected: No skill access, no actions (can’t expedite or create POs from chat), no multi-step workflows. Limited to read-only data queries.

SELECTED   Dual mode: Cortex Analyst (fast) + full agent (when needed)

Default to Cortex Analyst for data queries (fast responses). Escalate to SUPPLY_CHAIN_AGENT when the query involves skills or actions. The app detects intent from the query.

Why selected: Fast for most questions. Full agent capability when needed (expedite, reorder, forecast). Best of both worlds without forcing every question through the full agent pipeline.

Decision: Chart library

REJECTED   Altair

Declarative, cleaner syntax, good for simple charts.

Why rejected: Less capable for interactive features needed here — hover tooltips on multi-series trends, zoom on time-series, click-through on heatmaps, cost breakdown drilldowns.

SELECTED   Plotly

Interactive charts with hover tooltips, zoom, click events. Handles multi-series comparisons and cost breakdowns well.

Why selected: Better fit for supply chain dashboards — OTD trend hover shows exact values, cost breakdown bars are clickable, heatmaps support zoom. Works in SiS via st.plotly_chart.

Decision: Conversation persistence

REJECTED   Backend persistence (write to table)

Store conversation history in a Snowflake table for cross-session recall.

Why rejected: Adds write operations, another table to manage, and privacy concerns (who can see whose conversations). Not needed for the use case.

SELECTED   Frontend-only persistence (st.session_state)

Conversation history stored in st.session_state. Survives page navigation within the same session. Clears on browser refresh or session end.

Why selected: No backend writes. No extra objects. Sufficient for within-session continuity. User can reference earlier answers while navigating between pages.

16. CoCo Automations

3 team-based daily briefs deployed as Snowflake AGENT TASKs via cortex automation create. Each runs unattended on a weekday schedule and produces a status report for its team. Staggered timing ensures Logistics feeds Planning feeds Procurement.

AutomationTeamCadenceFires At (IST)What It Reports
LOGISTICS_DAILY_BRIEF Logistics Weekdays 7:30 AM In-transit shipment status (ON_TRACK/AT_RISK/DELAYED/CRITICAL), carrier OTD snapshot, open expedite tracker, order impact from delayed shipments
PLANNING_DAILY_BRIEF Planning Weekdays 8:00 AM Inventory health by risk bucket (CRITICAL/WARNING/WATCH/HEALTHY), order fulfillment status, demand vs stock gap analysis, forecast model staleness check
PROCUREMENT_DAILY_BRIEF Procurement Weekdays 8:30 AM Supplier scorecard ranking + underperformer flags, landed cost trends (7-day vs 30-day), pending reorder candidates with best supplier, open procurement alerts

Decision: Automation architecture

REJECTED   Single unified daily report for all teams

One automation producing a combined report with all metrics.

Why rejected: Different teams care about different metrics. A combined report is noisy — procurement doesn't need shipment tracking details, logistics doesn't need cost trend analysis. Team-specific briefs are more actionable.

SELECTED   Team-based daily briefs with staggered timing

3 separate automations, one per team: Logistics (7:30 AM), Planning (8:00 AM), Procurement (8:30 AM). Staggered so shipment status informs inventory analysis, which informs reorder decisions.

Why selected: Each team gets exactly the metrics they need. Staggered timing creates an information cascade — Logistics status is fresh when Planning reads inventory, and Planning gaps are visible when Procurement evaluates reorders. All are read-only (default RSS), results in transcript + email.

REJECTED   Write-enabled automations that auto-create POs or expedites

Allow automations to directly call CREATE_PURCHASE_ORDER or TRIGGER_EXPEDITE.

Why rejected: Unattended writes are dangerous. Automations would need --without-read-only which drops all safety guardrails. Write actions should require human confirmation via the agent or Streamlit interface. Automations detect and report — humans decide and act.
